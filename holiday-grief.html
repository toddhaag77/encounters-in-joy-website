Grief and the holidays · Barrow County, GA

Getting through the holidays after a loss.

When someone is missing, the season everyone calls merry can be the hardest stretch of the year. Here are eight things that help — and a free place to spend an afternoon with people who understand.

Read the guide Free grief group dates

The first holidays after a loss are a calendar of firsts — and nobody hands you a map.

The holidays are built around people. The chair at the table, the voice on the phone Christmas morning, the one who always made the dressing — when they're gone, every tradition points at the empty space. Add the pressure to be cheerful, and it's no wonder so many people dread November and December.

If you're dreading it too, nothing is wrong with you. Grief gets louder when the world gets festive. Whether your loss was this year or many years ago, that's normal.

A gentle guide

Eight things that help.

Take what's useful and leave the rest. There's no right way to do this.

  1. 1

    Decide ahead of time, not in the moment

    Look at the calendar and choose which gatherings you'll go to and which you'll skip. Drive yourself, so you can leave when you need to. Having a plan takes some of the dread out of the day.

  2. 2

    Give yourself permission to change traditions

    You don't have to do everything the way you always have. Eat somewhere different, open gifts at a different time, or skip the big dinner this year. Traditions will wait for you — you can bring them back when you're ready.

  3. 3

    Say their name

    Friends often avoid mentioning the person you lost because they're afraid of upsetting you. It can help to go first. Tell a story, set out a photo, light a candle, or make their favorite dish.

  4. 4

    Plan for the hardest days

    Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve, New Year's, birthdays, and anniversaries tend to hit hardest. Decide who you'll be with and what you'll do. The days leading up are often harder than the day itself.

  5. 5

    Tell one or two people exactly what you need

    People want to help but don't know how. Be specific: “Will you sit with me at the Christmas Eve service?” or “Can you call me Christmas morning?”

  6. 6

    Take care of your body

    Grief is exhausting. Protect your sleep, eat real meals, get outside for a walk when you can, and go easy on alcohol — it tends to make low days lower.

  7. 7

    Make room for joy without guilt

    Laughing at a family story isn't betrayal. Grief and gladness can sit at the same table, sometimes in the same hour.

  8. 8

    Don't carry it alone

    Call a friend, talk to your pastor, come to a support group, or sit down with a counselor. You don't need the right words. You only need to show up.

If faith is part of your life, it's all right to bring your grief to God honestly. The Psalms are full of people who did.

Pastors, funeral homes, and friends are welcome to print it and pass it along.

One-on-one counseling

When it might be time to talk with someone.

Grief isn't something to fix, and there's no deadline for it. But sometimes it gets heavier than one person can carry. It may help to talk with a counselor if, for weeks at a time:

  • you're struggling to get through work or ordinary days
  • you can't sleep, or you can't stop sleeping
  • you've pulled away from almost everyone
  • you feel numb, or the guilt won't let up
  • you're leaning on alcohol or pills to get through the evenings

Reverend Jeff Langley and Stephanie Langley meet with people in person at our Statham office or by Zoom. There's a set fee per session, due at the time of service, with a sliding scale and Counseling Scholarship Fund for anyone who needs it. More about grief counseling.
